01

Day and night are not enough

At first, people understood only two repeating things: day and night. That was enough to decide when to work, when to rest, and when to sleep.

But life needed longer plans. When to hunt, when to gather in large groups, when it is safe to travel. None of that fits inside a single day. People needed a longer time system, and the only thing they could use as a reference was the sky.

// analogy

Think of a clock that only has a second hand. It tells you the time is passing, but you can’t plan a meeting with it. A calendar is the minute and hour hands people had to invent next.

02

The seven-day week came from the sky

Looking up, people noticed bright objects that floated and never fell. Early cultures treated them as gods watching from above. The ones visible to the naked eye became the basis of the week.

Planets that cannot be seen without a telescope, such as Pluto, never received a day.

A week gave people a rhythm longer than a day: when to rest, when to work, even when to worship. It was a clear improvement, but still too short to say when to hunt or when winter arrives.

03

The lunar system: a 29.5-day cycle

The Moon is visible from everywhere and changes shape: crescent, growing to full, shrinking back to crescent, over and over. That repetition makes it a natural clock. One full cycle always takes 29.5 days.

What the phases meant in practice

  • Full moon: bright roads. Safe to travel between towns, since there were no lamps, and a good time for big gatherings.
  • Waning moon: it will soon go dark. Finish your journey; dark roads meant danger from animals and bandits.
// analogy

The Moon is a street light that slowly turns itself on and off over a month. Travelers planned around it the way we plan around a bus timetable.

// the limit

The Moon’s cycle says nothing about seasons. A 29.5-day clock cannot tell you when winter starts or when to plant.

04

The solar system: a 365-day cycle

Seasons exist, and ignoring them is dangerous. Before winter, people had to hunt and dry food or they might starve. Farmers had to know when to plant and when to harvest. A longer reference was needed, and again it was in the sky.

Look at the Sun at the same clock time every day. Its position does not stay put: it shifts a little each day, tracing a pattern (the analemma) and finally arriving back where it started. By observation, that took 365 days. We call that cycle a year.

With this solar system, people could tell when winter was coming and prepare. It was a reference that supported civilization from then on.

// analogy

If the Moon is the minute hand, the Sun is the hour hand of the sky. It moves more slowly, but it is the one that tells you which part of the year you are in.

05

Moon or Sun: which is better?

The honest answer is: it depends on what you need.

travel between townsgood: full moon = lightnot helpful

when seasons arrivecannot predictgood
planting, harvestcannot predictgood

History does favor the solar system: a longer span means you can measure time further ahead, predict seasons, and plan work and trade. That is a big reason civilization advanced.

// human habit

People were already used to the Moon, and people tend to resist changing a system they know, even when a better one exists. So instead of switching, they tried to make the lunar system represent the solar year. That led to lunisolar calendars.

06

Lunisolar: making the Moon fit the Sun

The idea: one year is 12 lunar months. But does that match the solar year?

// 12 lunar months
12 × 29.5 = 354 days
// one solar year
365 days
→ 11 days short, every year

The fix: look for a longer match

If one year doesn’t line up, try a longer span. Over 19 years, the solar side is 19 × 365 = 6,935 days. The lunar side, 19 × 12 = 228 months × 29.5, is only 6,726 days. The gap is 209 days, about 7 months.

The correction follows directly: add 7 extra months spread across each 19-year cycle. That is what makes the calendar lunisolar.

// lab 1 · how the gap grows
// analogy

Two runners lap a track at slightly different speeds. After one lap you barely notice the gap; after nineteen laps they are clearly out of step. Adding extra months is like making the slower runner take a shortcut to catch up.

07

Julian calendar: a year is 365.25 days

Julius Caesar, working before the common era, tracked the Sun’s position using shadows on a sundial. He noticed something odd: after 365 days the Sun was close to its starting place but not exactly there. The second year, closer. The third, closer still. After the fourth year, it landed exactly on the first year’s spot.

So the true cycle is not 365 days. It is 365.25, because 0.25 × 4 = 1.

  • Problem: the real year is longer than 365 days by 0.25 day.
  • Fix: add 1 day every 4 years.
  • Those extra-day years are called leap years.
// analogy

Imagine a watch that loses a quarter of a minute every day. You don’t fix it daily. You push it forward by a full minute every four days. The leap day is the same idea.

08

Gregorian calendar: fixing the fix

For about 1,600 years the Julian calendar worked well enough. Then, in 1582, Pope Gregory XIII (with better measuring tools) found that the year is actually 365.2425 days, not 365.25.

The difference is tiny: 365.25 − 365.2425 = 0.0075 day per year. But it accumulates.

// Julian over-counts by
0.0075 day / year
1 ÷ 0.0075 ≈ 133 years per extra day
// over 400 years
≈ 3 extra days

If the calendar runs ahead of the Sun, dates drift away from the seasons, and even Christmas stops falling in the right season. Gregory’s idea was to spread the three surplus days across 400 years: remove a leap day in the years 100, 200 and 300, but not in year 400.

The rule

function isLeapYear(year) {
  if (year % 400 === 0) return true;   // 2000 → leap
  if (year % 100 === 0) return false;  // 1900 → not leap
  return year % 4 === 0;                    // 2024 → leap
}
// lab 2 · is it a leap year?
// the day Britain lost

When Britain moved from the Julian to the Gregorian calendar, it had to skip 11 days to catch up with the Sun. People who went to bed on 2 September woke up on 14 September. The days are not lost in reality, only in the calendar.

The Gregorian calendar is the most widely used calendar in the world today.

09

A calendar is not an era

A question from the Q&A: is “Masehi” (the Christian era) the same thing as the Gregorian calendar? No.

// Gregorian
a CALENDAR:
an algorithm that keeps
dates aligned with the Sun
(365.2425 days / year)
// Masehi / CE
an ERA:
a way of numbering years
(before / after a chosen
starting point)

Because the world has many religions, the labels BC (“Before Christ”) and AD (“Anno Domini”) are often replaced by BCE (“Before Common Era”) and CE (“Common Era”). The year numbers stay the same; only the wording is neutral.

OlderNeutralMeaning
BCBCEbefore the common era
ADCEthe common era
// analogy

A calendar is the ruler. An era is where you decide to put the zero mark. You can measure the same distances with the same ruler and still label the starting point differently.
